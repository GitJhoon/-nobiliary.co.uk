Royal & noble houses

From Wessex to Windsor

The royal dynasties and noble families that shaped Britain: their territories, defining moments and most notable members.

W
Royal dynasty

House of Wessex

c. 6th century–1066 (with interruptions)

Provided the political core from which the kingdom of England was unified.

Wessex · England · 4 notable members · 5 key events

Read the story

The West Saxon royal line rose from one of the major Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. Under Ecgberht and his descendants Wessex became the leading power resisting Scandinavian conquest. Alfred the Great preserved the kingdom during the Viking crisis, while Edward the Elder and Æthelstan expanded West Saxon power over most of England. Æthelstan is commonly treated as the first ruler of a unified kingdom of England.

The dynasty returned after the Danish kings with Edward the Confessor, but his death without children in 1066 produced the succession crisis that ended in the Norman Conquest.

Royal dynasty

House of Knýtlinga (Danish Royal House)

c. 10th–11th centuries; ruled England 1013–1014 and 1016–1042

Connected England to a Scandinavian North Sea empire.

Denmark · England · Norway · 3 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

The Danish conquest placed England inside a North Sea monarchy. Sweyn Forkbeard briefly displaced Æthelred in 1013, and his son Cnut completed the conquest in 1016. Cnut ruled England alongside Denmark and later Norway, governing through both Scandinavian networks and established English institutions.

After Cnut's death, Harold Harefoot and Harthacnut ruled in turn. Harthacnut's death in 1042 allowed Edward the Confessor to restore the West Saxon line.

G
Royal & noble house

House of Godwin

11th century; royal in 1066

Shows the extraordinary power that late Anglo-Saxon earls could accumulate.

Wessex · England · Northumbria · 4 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

Godwin, Earl of Wessex, built one of the most powerful aristocratic families in eleventh-century England. His daughter Edith married Edward the Confessor, and his sons held major earldoms. Harold Godwinson became the dominant magnate and was chosen king in January 1066.

The family's rapid rise also created enemies. Harold defeated Harald Hardrada at Stamford Bridge but was killed weeks later at Hastings, ending the family's brief possession of the crown.

N
Royal dynasty

House of Normandy

1066–1135 as direct ruling line in England

The Norman Conquest replaced much of the Anglo-Saxon elite with Norman lords.

Normandy · England · 4 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

William, Duke of Normandy, conquered England in 1066 and created a new cross-Channel aristocratic order. The dynasty transformed landholding, castle building, church leadership, language and elite culture while retaining and developing important elements of Anglo-Saxon royal government.

William II and Henry I followed. The death of Henry's only legitimate son William Adelin in the White Ship disaster created a succession problem that led to the Anarchy between Stephen and Empress Matilda.

B
Royal dynasty

House of Blois

1135–1154 in England

Its English reign is inseparable from the Anarchy.

Blois · England · 2 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

Stephen of Blois seized the English throne after Henry I's death despite earlier oaths recognizing Empress Matilda. His accession triggered the civil war known as the Anarchy. Neither side achieved decisive control for years.

The conflict ended when Stephen recognized Matilda's son Henry as his heir. Stephen's death in 1154 therefore transferred the crown to the Angevin/Plantagenet line.

Stephen
P
Royal dynasty

House of Plantagenet

1154–1399 in the direct royal line; cadet branches ruled to 1485

One of the longest and most consequential royal dynasties in English history.

England · Anjou · Normandy · Aquitaine · Wales · Ireland · 5 notable members · 5 key events

Read the story

The Plantagenets ruled England for nearly two and a half centuries if the Lancastrian and Yorkist cadet branches are included. Henry II inherited a broad collection of territories often called the Angevin Empire and reshaped royal justice. Later kings faced Magna Carta, baronial wars, conquest in Wales, war in Scotland, the Hundred Years' War and the Black Death.

Edward III's sons created powerful cadet branches, above all Lancaster and York. Their rival claims ultimately helped produce the Wars of the Roses.

Royal dynasty

House of Lancaster

1399–1461 and 1470–1471

Created a new precedent for deposition and parliamentary recognition of a claimant.

England · Lancaster · 4 notable members · 5 key events

Read the story

The House of Lancaster came to the throne when Henry Bolingbroke deposed Richard II in 1399 and became Henry IV. Henry V's victory at Agincourt and the Treaty of Troyes temporarily made the dynasty appear dominant in both England and France.

Under Henry VI, English power in France collapsed and factional politics hardened into the Wars of the Roses. Henry VI was deposed by Edward IV, briefly restored in 1470 and removed again in 1471.

Y
Royal dynasty

House of York

1461–1470 and 1471–1485

Central protagonist in the Wars of the Roses.

England · York · 5 notable members · 5 key events

Read the story

The House of York challenged Lancastrian rule during the Wars of the Roses. Edward IV won the crown after victories including Towton, was briefly expelled in 1470, and returned the next year. His unexpected death in 1483 triggered another crisis involving his sons, the Princes in the Tower, and his brother Richard III.

Richard III's defeat and death at Bosworth in 1485 ended Yorkist rule. Henry VII then married Elizabeth of York, joining the principal rival claims in the Tudor dynasty.

T
Royal dynasty

House of Tudor

1485–1603

Ended the main cycle of Wars of the Roses dynastic conflict.

Wales · England · Ireland · 4 notable members · 5 key events

Read the story

Henry Tudor won the throne at Bosworth in 1485 and married Elizabeth of York, combining rival dynastic claims. The Tudors strengthened the monarchy after the Wars of the Roses and presided over the English Reformation, the dissolution of the monasteries, closer legal integration of Wales with England and the expansion of maritime ambition.

All three legitimate children of Henry VIII ruled. Elizabeth I died childless in 1603, ending the dynasty and allowing James VI of Scotland, a Stuart descendant of Henry VII's daughter Margaret, to inherit the English crown.

S
Royal dynasty

House of Stuart

1371–1714 in Scotland; 1603–1714 in England/Great Britain with interruption 1649–1660

United the crowns of England and Scotland in one monarch in 1603.

Scotland · England · Ireland · Great Britain · 6 notable members · 6 key events

Read the story

The Stewarts first became kings of Scotland in 1371. In 1603 James VI of Scotland inherited the English and Irish crowns as James I, creating a personal union of the crowns while the kingdoms remained legally separate. Stuart disputes over monarchy, religion and taxation contributed to the Wars of the Three Kingdoms, the execution of Charles I and the republican Interregnum.

The dynasty returned in 1660, but James II was overthrown in 1688. Mary II and Anne continued the Stuart bloodline on the throne. Under Anne, the Acts of Union created the Kingdom of Great Britain in 1707. Her death without surviving children triggered the Hanoverian succession.

O
Royal dynasty

House of Orange-Nassau

1689–1702 in Britain (jointly with Mary II until 1694)

Central to the Glorious Revolution of 1688–89.

Dutch Republic · England · Scotland · Ireland · 1 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

William of Orange invaded England in 1688 after being invited by leading opponents of James II. Parliament offered the crown jointly to William and his wife Mary, James II's Protestant daughter. Their reign became the constitutional settlement known as the Glorious Revolution.

William's rule tied England more firmly to European coalition warfare against Louis XIV and coincided with institutional changes including the Bill of Rights and the growth of public credit.

William III
H
Royal dynasty

House of Hanover

1714–1901 in Great Britain/United Kingdom

The succession confirmed the Protestant settlement established by Parliament.

Hanover · Great Britain · United Kingdom · 5 notable members · 6 key events

Read the story

George I inherited the British throne in 1714 as the senior eligible Protestant descendant of James I. The Hanoverians ruled during the rise of cabinet government, the Jacobite rebellions, global imperial expansion, the American Revolution, the wars with Revolutionary and Napoleonic France and the industrial transformation of Britain.

Victoria was the last British sovereign of the House of Hanover. Because Hanover followed male-only succession, the personal union with the German kingdom ended when she became queen in 1837. Her son Edward VII belonged to his father's House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha.

S
Royal dynasty

House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha

1901–1917 under that name in Britain

Connected the British monarchy to a wider European dynastic network.

United Kingdom · Saxe-Coburg and Gotha · 3 notable members · 3 key events

Read the story

Queen Victoria belonged to the House of Hanover, but her son Edward VII took the dynastic house of his father, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. Edward VII and the first seven years of George V's reign therefore belonged to this house.

During the First World War, anti-German sentiment made the dynasty's German name politically awkward. In July 1917 George V adopted Windsor as the official name of the British royal house.

W
Royal dynasty

House of Windsor

1917–present

Recast the monarchy's public identity during the First World War.

United Kingdom · Commonwealth realms · 5 notable members · 5 key events

Read the story

The House of Windsor was created in 1917 when George V replaced the German dynastic name Saxe-Coburg and Gotha during the First World War. The house has since encompassed the abdication crisis, the Second World War, decolonization, the transformation of the British Empire into the Commonwealth and the long reign of Elizabeth II.

Charles III succeeded Elizabeth II in 2022. The royal house remains Windsor. Mountbatten-Windsor is a surname used in specified circumstances by descendants of Elizabeth II and Prince Philip; it did not replace Windsor as the name of the royal house.

B
Noble house

House of Beaufort

late 14th–16th centuries as a major dynastic force

Supplied senior Lancastrian commanders and churchmen.

England · Wales · 3 notable members · 3 key events

Read the story

The Beauforts descended from John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, and Katherine Swynford. Their children were legitimized, although later royal succession claims were politically contested. The family became one of the great Lancastrian affinities of the fifteenth century.

Margaret Beaufort's son Henry Tudor used his Beaufort descent as part of his claim to the English crown. His victory at Bosworth founded the Tudor dynasty, making the Beaufort connection one of the decisive genealogical bridges between Plantagenet and Tudor rule.

N
Noble house

House of Neville

medieval–early modern; peak influence in the 15th century

Their northern affinity made them major rivals of the Percys.

Northern England · Yorkshire · County Durham · Warwick · 3 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

The Nevilles became one of the most powerful noble families in late medieval England. Through marriage they acquired the earldom of Warwick and became deeply entangled in the dynastic politics of the Wars of the Roses.

Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, known as the 'Kingmaker', helped Edward IV win the throne, later turned against him, restored Henry VI and was killed at Barnet in 1471. Neville women also connected the family directly to Yorkist royalty.

P
Noble house

House of Percy

11th century–present

Dominated politics and military organization on the northern frontier for centuries.

Northumberland · Yorkshire · Sussex · 3 notable members · 3 key events

Read the story

The Percys accumulated estates in northern England after the Norman Conquest and became central defenders and power-brokers along the Anglo-Scottish border. They purchased Alnwick Castle in 1309, which became one of the family's principal seats.

Their influence repeatedly brought them into conflict with kings and rival magnates. The Percy rebellions against Henry IV, including the revolt associated with Henry 'Hotspur' Percy, became some of the most famous noble challenges of the late Middle Ages.

H
Noble house

House of Howard

15th century–present

One of the most important surviving English ducal families.

Norfolk · Sussex · Surrey · England · 4 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

The Howards rose to national prominence in the late fifteenth century and became one of the most durable aristocratic families of Tudor and later England. John Howard was created Duke of Norfolk by Richard III and was killed with him at Bosworth.

The family repeatedly occupied the highest levels of court, military and religious politics. Thomas Howard, 3rd Duke of Norfolk, was uncle to Anne Boleyn and Catherine Howard. The Howards also held the hereditary office of Earl Marshal, linking the family closely to state ceremonial and heraldry.

B
Royal dynasty

House of Bruce

Royal in Scotland 1306–1371 through the direct Bruce line

Central dynasty of the Wars of Scottish Independence.

Scotland · Annandale · 3 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

The Bruce family rose from Anglo-Norman origins to become one of the principal claimants to the Scottish crown during the Wars of Scottish Independence. Robert Bruce was crowned King Robert I in 1306, defeated Edward II's army at Bannockburn in 1314 and secured international recognition of Scottish kingship.

Robert's son David II died without children in 1371. The crown then passed through Robert's daughter Marjorie Bruce to her son Robert Stewart, beginning the royal House of Stewart.

D
Noble house

House of Douglas

12th century–present through branches

One of medieval Scotland's dominant magnate houses.

Scotland · Lanarkshire · Borders · 3 notable members · 3 key events

Read the story

The Douglases rose in the Wars of Scottish Independence and became one of medieval Scotland's most powerful aristocratic houses. Sir James Douglas was a close lieutenant of Robert the Bruce, and later generations accumulated major territorial and military influence.

By the fifteenth century the 'Black Douglases' could rival royal power, prompting violent conflict with James II. Other branches, including the 'Red Douglases' of Angus, remained influential in Scottish and later British politics.

C
Noble house

House of Campbell

medieval–present

A major bridge between Highland regional power and the Scottish state.

Argyll · Scottish Highlands · Scotland · 2 notable members · 3 key events

Read the story

The Campbells expanded from Argyll to become one of the most influential Scottish noble families. Their chiefs built royal alliances and administrative power, and the earldom of Argyll became one of the kingdom's great magnate titles.

During the Reformation, civil wars and later union politics, Campbells repeatedly played major roles. The family also illustrates how Highland kinship, landed lordship and national political office overlapped.

S
Noble house

House of Spencer

15th century–present

Illustrates the rise of a wealthy landowning family into the peerage.

Northamptonshire · England · 3 notable members · 3 key events

Read the story

The Spencer family rose through sheep farming, land acquisition and court service, establishing Althorp as a principal seat in the early sixteenth century. Over time the family entered the peerage and became deeply connected to Whig politics and the British aristocracy.

The wider Spencer family includes the Spencer-Churchill line of the Dukes of Marlborough. In the twentieth century Diana Spencer's marriage to Charles, Prince of Wales, connected the family directly to the modern royal succession through Princes William and Harry.

C
Noble house

House of Cavendish

16th century–present

A leading Whig aristocratic dynasty from the seventeenth century onward.

Derbyshire · England · Ireland · 4 notable members · 4 key events

Read the story

The Cavendish family's great rise began with Sir William Cavendish and Bess of Hardwick. Their descendants built one of Britain's most important aristocratic dynasties, centred on Chatsworth. The family became Earls and then Dukes of Devonshire and was deeply involved in Whig politics.

William Cavendish, 4th Earl of Devonshire, was one of the 'Immortal Seven' who invited William of Orange to intervene in 1688 and was created Duke of Devonshire in 1694. Later Cavendishes served as prime minister, senior ministers, governors and major patrons of science and culture.

E
Noble house

House of Egerton

16th century–present through three main branches

A Cheshire family whose branches held the dukedom of Bridgewater, the earldoms of Wilton, Ellesmere and Egerton, and one of England's oldest baronetcies.

Cheshire · Bridgewater · Ellesmere · Tatton Park · England · 3 branches

Read the story

Grey Egerton branch. Sir Roland Egerton was created a baronet of Egerton and Oulton in 1617 and married Bridget Grey, a coheiress of the Barons Grey de Wilton. Their descendant Sir Thomas Grey Egerton, 7th Baronet (1749–1814), was made Baron Grey de Wilton in 1784 and Earl of Wilton in 1801, with the titles passing through his daughter Eleanor to the younger sons of her husband Robert Grosvenor, 1st Marquess of Westminster. The baronetcy went to a kinsman, and the Grey Egerton baronets continue to the present day.

Bridgewater branch. Thomas Egerton (1540–1617), illegitimate son of Sir Richard Egerton of Ridley, rose to become Lord Chancellor and was created Baron Ellesmere (1603) and Viscount Brackley (1616). His son John became 1st Earl of Bridgewater in 1617, and Scroop Egerton was raised to Duke of Bridgewater in 1720. Francis, 3rd Duke (1736–1803), built the Bridgewater Canal; at his death the dukedom became extinct, and the earldom ended with the 8th Earl in 1829.

Ellesmere and Sutherland. Lord Francis Leveson-Gower, a son of the 1st Duke of Sutherland, inherited the Bridgewater estates in 1833, took the surname Egerton and was created Earl of Ellesmere in 1846. In 1963 the 5th Earl of Ellesmere also succeeded as 6th Duke of Sutherland, and both titles remain united.

Tatton branch. Descended in the female line from a younger son of the 2nd Earl of Bridgewater, the Egertons of Tatton Park in Cheshire were Barons Egerton from 1859 to 1958. Wilbraham Egerton was also created Earl Egerton in 1897, a title that ended with his death in 1909.
